The fingerspelling alphabet

Fingerspelling is how ASL spells out an English word, one handshape per letter. It is used for names, places, and words that have no sign of their own.

⚠
No video of the letters yet, on purpose. We only show footage of a real person signing. We checked the letter entries in the Internet Archive dictionary on 6 October 2026: they are computer-drawn hands, not a person, so they are not shown here. Until a real signer is filmed, each letter is described in words. A friend or teacher who signs is the best check.

Practise with yourself

Pick a letter from the list below or let the page choose. Make the handshape, check it against the description, ideally in a mirror, and be honest with yourself.

This is a practice aid, not a teacher. Nothing here watches you: there is no camera, and nothing you do is recorded or sent anywhere.

Make this letter

Two letters move: J and Z

Every letter except J and Z is a handshape you hold still. J is the I handshape drawing a J in the air with the little finger. Z is the index finger drawing a Z. A held picture or a word description cannot show a movement properly, so these two are listed but left out of the drill until there is a real clip of them.

Letters that look alike

These are the ones beginners mix up. The difference is usually small: where the thumb sits, or which way the hand points.

  • A S T M N E — all closed hands. The thumb tells them apart: beside the fist (A), across the front (S), tucked under one finger (T), under two (N), under three (M), or under bent fingertips (E).
  • U V R — two fingers up: together (U), apart (V), crossed (R).
  • K V — K has the thumb tip touching the middle finger between the two raised fingers; V does not.
  • K P — the same shape; P points down.
  • G Q — the same shape; Q points down.
  • G H — both point sideways; G with one finger, H with two.
  • C O — C is open; O closes so the fingertips meet the thumb.
  • I J — J is I plus a movement.

Good habits from the start

  • Think of the word, not the letters. As you spell, say the word to yourself the way it sounds, rather than naming each letter.
  • Between two spelled words, leave a tiny pause and move the hand very slightly to the side.
  • When you meet the Deaf community, spell your name. Do not make up your own name sign — name signs are usually given to you by Deaf people.

Fingerspelling technique and the name-sign custom: Dr. Bill Vicars, Lifeprint — Fingerspelling. Read his lessons there; they are not copied here.

ASL and Deaf culture: the basics

Written with input from a hearing signer. For anything here, the best teachers are Deaf people. Most of the sources below are Deaf-led — the National Association of the Deaf, Gallaudet University, and Dr. Bill Vicars, a Deaf ASL instructor — and we recommend going to them directly.

ASL is its own language

American Sign Language is not English on the hands. It is a complete, natural language with its own grammar and word order, separate from English.

It is not universal either. Different countries have different sign languages, and ASL has regional accents of its own.

Source: NIDCD — American Sign Language (US National Institutes of Health).

Fingerspelling vs signs

Most of ASL is signs: one sign carries a whole idea. Fingerspelling is a smaller part of the language, used to spell English words — names, places, and words without a sign.

A beginner who only fingerspells is spelling English slowly, not speaking ASL. Learn signs early.

Source: NIDCD; Lifeprint — Fingerspelling.

The face is grammar

ASL is expressed with the face as well as the hands. Facial expression and head movement — called non-manual markers — change what a sentence means.

Raised eyebrows mark a yes/no question. Lowered, drawn-together eyebrows mark a question with who, what, where, when, why or how. A head shake makes a sentence negative. Mouth shapes can add size or manner. Signing with a blank face is like speaking in a flat monotone, and can say something different from what you meant.

Sources: NIDCD; Dr. Bill Vicars, Lifeprint — Non-manual markers.

Deaf with a capital D

Many people write Deaf, with a capital D, for the cultural community that shares ASL, history and values, and deaf, lower case, for not hearing. Not every deaf person is part of Deaf culture, and people choose how they identify.

ASL is the backbone of American Deaf culture. Avoid “hearing impaired” and “deaf-mute”: the National Association of the Deaf explains why most of the community rejects both.

Sources: NAD — American Sign Language; NAD — Community and Culture FAQ; the capital-D convention is usually credited to linguist James Woodward (1972), see Deaf culture.

Everyday etiquette

Getting attention: wave if the person can see you; a light tap on the shoulder if they can’t; flicking the lights a few times for a room.

Eye contact: keep it. Everything in a signed conversation comes through the eyes, so looking away is like walking off mid-sentence.

Turns: the signer has the floor until they show they have finished, with a pause or a look.

Source: Gallaudet University, Laurent Clerc National Deaf Education Center — American Deaf Culture.

How to learn well

Take a class, ideally taught by a Deaf teacher. Gallaudet University, the university for Deaf and hard of hearing students, runs ASL Connect online.

Find your local Deaf community — Deaf clubs, events, ASL socials — and go, mostly to watch and be patient. A state association of the Deaf (listed by the NAD) is a good place to ask.

Free lessons from a Deaf instructor: Dr. Bill Vicars’ Lifeprint (ASL University).

How this is made

Real people, or nothing

Guard.IAN does not invent a sign and does not render one. He plays recorded footage of a human signer, or he shows nothing.

No 3D models, no avatars, no characters driven by motion capture, no AI-generated people. An AI guessing at a sign teaches somebody something wrong, and a wrong sign is worse than no sign.

Checked before shown

On 5 October 2026 this page briefly showed an alphabet made from a 3D model and called it a person. It was taken down within the hour and logged on the corrections page.

On 6 October we checked the Internet Archive’s letter entries frame by frame before using them. They are computer-drawn hands, so they are not shown. The word clips in the dictionary are a real signer.

Nothing is watching you

No camera, no account, no record of what you look up or practise.

The dictionary asks the Internet Archive for one clip at a time. Nothing else leaves your browser.

Licences respected

Most ASL video collections are for research only, because Deaf signers agreed to research and not to somebody’s product. Those are not used here, and licence terms are not stretched.

The dictionary clips are marked public domain by the organisation that recorded them.
